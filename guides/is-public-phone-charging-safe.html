Is public phone charging safe? What the FBI warning actually means

You've seen the headlines: the FBI and TSA say don't plug your phone into public USB ports. The technique they're describing — "juice jacking" — is real. The risk to an up-to-date iPhone is small, and it's worth understanding why, because the honest answer is more useful than the scary one.

The short answer

Juice jacking is a real, demonstrated attack: a modified USB port or cable that tries to pull data from your phone or push malware onto it while it charges. It's been shown at security conferences since 2011. But after more than a decade of warnings, there are no publicly documented, confirmed cases of a traveller being compromised by a public charger. The FBI's 2023 warning and the TSA's 2025 one are precautionary — sensible advice about a possible attack, not a response to a wave of victims.

Use your own charger and a wall outlet, or a power bank. That advice is correct, it's free, and it makes the whole question moot. But if you've ever plugged into an airport USB port and are now worried — the odds that anything happened are very low, and lower still if your iPhone is up to date.

Why iPhones are hard to juice-jack

USB carries power and data down the same cable, which is the whole premise of the attack. But an iPhone doesn't hand over data just because something asks:

  • "Trust This Computer?" Since 2013, an iPhone treats every new USB connection as hostile until you say otherwise. Plug into anything that tries to talk data and you get that prompt. Tap "Don't Trust" — or ignore it — and the port delivers power only. Nothing moves.
  • USB Restricted Mode. Since 2018, if your iPhone has been locked for about an hour, its data connection over the port switches off entirely — a cable left plugged into a sleeping phone gets electricity and nothing else.
  • iOS 18.4's stronger gate. In 2025, researchers at Graz University of Technology demonstrated "ChoiceJacking" — malicious hardware that could fake the tap on the Trust prompt, defeating the old defence. Apple's answer, in iOS 18.4, is that authorising a new USB data connection now requires your passcode or Face ID, not just a tap. A charger can't fake that. If your iPhone is on iOS 18.4 or later, all publicly known juice-jacking techniques are blocked.

The honest caveat: this protection depends on your iPhone being updated. An old iPhone on an old iOS has weaker defences, and that's the one where the warnings genuinely bite.

What's overstated

You'll read that a compromised port can "steal your photos and passwords in seconds" or "install tracking software while you grab a coffee." Against an updated iPhone that hasn't been unlocked and told to trust the port, neither happens. You'll also read about "video jacking" — hidden cameras in charging kiosks recording your screen. That's a real shoulder-surfing risk, but it's about what you type and view at the kiosk, not about your camera roll. And a public charger cannot see photos on a phone that isn't sending them.

The gap between "demonstrated at a security conference" and "happening to travellers at airports" is wide, and nobody selling a forty-dollar privacy gadget wants to explain it.

What actually helps

  • Carry your own charger and cable, or a power bank. A wall outlet is just electricity — there's no data pin to worry about. This is the FBI's advice and it's right, mostly because it removes the question rather than because the risk was high.
  • Keep iOS updated. iOS 18.4 or later closes the ChoiceJacking gap. If you're on something older, updating is a bigger safety step than any accessory.
  • Never tap "Trust" on a prompt you didn't expect. If a charger triggers "Trust This Computer?", that's the charger asking for data. The answer is no.
  • Don't use a cable you found or were given. A modified cable — rather than a port — is the most plausible way this attack reaches an ordinary traveller. Bring your own.
  • A "USB data blocker" — a small adapter that physically omits the data pins — works, but on an updated iPhone it's belt-and-braces, not a necessity.

So why is this on a camera-roll app's website? Because the charging-port scare is a good example of a privacy risk that's smaller than it sounds — and it's worth being clear about which risks are real. A public USB port almost certainly can't see your photos. A person holding your unlocked phone can. A border officer with your device can. The trade-in shop, the family member scrolling for one picture, the old backup on an old laptop — those are the real ways the photos in your camera roll get seen by someone else.

Worry about the phone in someone's hand, not the port on the wall.
