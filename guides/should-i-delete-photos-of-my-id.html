Guide · Your photos

Should I delete photos of my ID from my phone?

Mostly, yes. A photo of your passport, licence, or card usually did its job within minutes of being taken — and then stayed. Here's how to think about which to delete, which to keep, and how to keep the ones you need somewhere better than your camera roll.

Why the photo is there at all

Almost nobody photographs their ID for fun. It was for something: a landlord, a rental car, a flight booking, a form that wanted a number, a bank that wanted proof. You took the photo, sent or typed what was needed, and moved on. The photo's usefulness ended there. Its presence didn't — it settled into a library of thousands and became permanent by default. If you've had an iPhone for a few years, there's very likely more than one such photo in yours, and you probably couldn't say where.

Why it matters

A photo of your passport page or driving licence is close to a complete identity: full name, date of birth, document number, address, photo, sometimes signature. A payment card front is a card number; a card back is the security code you'd never write down. These are the exact things identity fraud is built from — and they're sitting in the one place on your phone that other people most often see. Not through hacking: through the phone being in someone else's hand — a repair, a family member scrolling, a colleague, a buyer, a border officer. And through the library's own reach: iCloud, the shared family library, the old backup on an old laptop.

Delete, keep, or move — a simple test

For each one you find, ask: would I need this exact photo again, and is my camera roll the right place for it?

  • Delete when the purpose is finished. The licence you photographed for a rental three years ago. The card photo from an online order. The passport page from a trip long past. The screenshot of a code that expired in ten minutes. This is most of them.
  • Move when you'll genuinely need it again but not here. Some people keep a copy of their passport for travel emergencies — sensible, but the camera roll (searchable, synced everywhere, first thing anyone scrolls) is the worst home for it. Better: iOS's Hidden album (Face ID-protected, out of the main grid), a password manager's secure notes, or a locked notes app. The photo survives; it stops being one swipe away.
  • Keep only what you'd want visible in your ordinary library. For ID photos that's rarely anything.

Two things worth knowing about deletion on an iPhone: it isn't instant — a deleted photo goes to Recently Deleted for 30 days, and you can empty that album to remove it now. And if iCloud Photos is on, deleting from one device deletes from all of them and from iCloud, which is what you want here.

What about the next time someone asks for a copy? You'll be asked again — a landlord, a host, an employer. The habit that helps: send it, then delete it from your camera roll the same day. And when you send it, send only what they need — cover the fields they have no use for, and mark the copy so you know where it went. Tools exist for exactly that step; it's a different job from finding the old copies, and it's worth doing well too.

What's overstated

You'll read that any photo of your ID on your phone is a catastrophe waiting to happen. It isn't — most sit unseen forever. The realistic frame is needless exposure: a photo carrying real risk and providing no benefit, in a place that's easy to see and easy to forget. Removing it doesn't require alarm; it just requires finding it.

You'll also read that "hidden" means safe. iOS's Hidden album is genuinely useful against casual browsing — but it's protection from a family member, not from a search. It's a better home than the main grid; it isn't a vault.

The hard part is finding them

The decision is easy once a photo is in front of you. The problem is that they aren't — they're scattered across years, they don't look like anything in a thumbnail, and Photos' search finds them inconsistently at best. Scrolling four thousand images to find the six that matter is not a thing anyone actually does. So the practical question isn't "should I delete them" — the answer's clear — it's "how do I find them without a weekend of scrolling."
