Before you sell or trade in your iPhone: what erasing it doesn't fix

Apple's "Erase All Content and Settings" does exactly what it says — the phone you hand over is genuinely clean. The catch is that your photos stopped living only on the phone years ago. Here's what the wipe handles, what it doesn't, and the one step almost everyone skips.

What the erase actually does

Settings → General → Transfer or Reset iPhone → Erase All Content and Settings removes everything from that device: photos, messages, apps, Apple Pay cards, accounts. Done correctly — with iCloud signed out and Find My turned off first — the phone is factory-fresh and Activation Lock is cleared for the next owner. Trade-in services reject devices where these steps were skipped, so the wipe itself is well-trodden and works. This isn't a story about a broken reset.

What it doesn't touch

Apple says it plainly: "Your content won't delete from iCloud when you erase your device." And that's the whole point of iCloud Photos — your library isn't on the phone, the phone is one window onto a library that lives in your account and appears on every device signed into it. So when you erase the old phone:

  • Your iCloud Photos library is untouched — every photo, including the ones you'd forgotten, is still there and appears on the new phone the moment you sign in.
  • Your backup carries everything forward. Set up the new phone from the old one's backup and the camera roll arrives intact — the passport photo from 2019 included. That's the feature: nothing lost across upgrades. It also means nothing forgotten is ever shed.
  • The old backup persists — in iCloud, or on a Mac or PC you backed up to years ago — as a complete copy of a library you may no longer look at.

So the erase protects the buyer from seeing your data. It does nothing about the fact that you are carrying every forgotten document photo forward, phone after phone, indefinitely.

The reframe. "Wipe before you sell" is about the phone leaving your hands. "Sweep before you migrate" is about what comes with you. Both matter; only the first is on anyone's checklist. The second is why a photo of your driving licence taken for a rental in 2019 is, right now, on your current phone, your iPad, and your iCloud — and will be on the next phone too.

The step most people skip: sweep before the backup

The moment before you back up the old phone is the last cheap moment to decide what carries forward. Anything you remove from your library then is gone from the backup, gone from iCloud (once Recently Deleted clears), and simply never arrives on the new phone. Anything you don't remove is inherited — and inherited photos are the ones nobody ever revisits.

Trade-in season is when people actually think about their phone's contents, which makes it the natural moment for the one thing the erase can't do: look at what's in the library, not just where the library lives.

The honest checklist

  1. Sweep the camera roll for the photos you'd rather not carry forward — old ID and passport photos, payment cards, screenshots of passwords and codes, private images. Keep what you need somewhere sensible; delete what's long finished its job. Do this before step 2.
  2. Back up (iCloud, or Finder/iTunes) — now the backup contains what you chose, not everything you ever photographed.
  3. Transfer to the new phone (Quick Start or from the backup).
  4. On the old phone: unpair your Apple Watch, sign out of iMessage and FaceTime, sign out of iCloud (Settings → your name → Sign Out), turn off Find My.
  5. Erase All Content and Settings — and if the phone uses an eSIM, choose to erase that too.
  6. Check at appleid.apple.com or Settings → your name that the old device no longer appears under your devices.

Steps 2–6 are Apple's own guidance and every trade-in guide repeats them. Step 1 is the one this page exists for.

What's overstated

You'll read that a factory reset "doesn't really delete anything" and that a buyer can recover your photos with the right software. On a modern iPhone this is essentially false: the storage is encrypted with a key that the erase destroys, so recovery from a properly erased device isn't a realistic threat. The residual risk isn't the buyer — it's the copies you kept.
