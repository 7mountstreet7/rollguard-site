Guide · Your photos

Who can see my photos when I hand over my phone?

Anyone holding it unlocked. That sounds obvious, and it is — but it's also the way camera-roll photos are actually seen by other people, far more than any hack, breach, or charging port. Here are the ordinary moments, what each one exposes, and what to do before they happen.

The moments

The repair

A cracked screen goes to a shop; the technician asks for your passcode to test the device after the fix. Reputable shops handle this professionally, and Apple's own repair process minimises it — but for the hours or days it's out of your hands, your library is available to whoever's working on it, and you can't see what they do. Photos are the first app anyone opens on an unlocked phone.

The work phone

A device your employer manages — or one you return when you leave — is theirs to inspect. IT tools can reach the camera roll, and shared or corporate backups carry it forward. Whatever's in your photos on that phone is, in practical terms, visible to your organisation.

The family

A child playing a game, a parent looking for one holiday photo, a partner checking a message — and one swipe past the picture they wanted is a picture they didn't. This is the most common exposure of all and the least malicious. iCloud Shared Photo Library and family iPads widen it further: what's in your library can be in theirs by design.

The buyer

If you sell or trade in without properly erasing, the next owner has everything. Modern iPhones erase properly, so this is avoidable — but the sweep that matters is before you back up, because what you don't remove comes with you to the next phone anyway.

The officer

At a border, an officer can ask to look through your phone — including your photos — without a warrant in Australia, the US, and the UK. It's rare, it's real, and it's exactly a stranger scrolling your library with legal authority.

The favour

"Can I borrow your phone for a second?" A friend making a call, a colleague checking a map, someone taking a group photo with your camera. The phone's unlocked; Photos is one tap away; most people are honest and some people are curious.

The pattern. None of these involves a hacker. Every one of them is a person, a phone, and a few seconds. That's why the charging-port warnings and the breach headlines are the wrong things to worry about for your camera roll, and why the honest question isn't "how do I stop the world seeing my phone" — it's "what's in my photos that I'd mind a person seeing, and why is it still there?"

What iOS protects, and what it doesn't

  • The lock screen protects everything — as long as the phone stays locked. The moment you unlock it and hand it over, the protection is gone. That's the whole problem in one sentence.
  • The Hidden album requires Face ID or your passcode to open, and hidden photos don't appear in the main grid, in albums, or in the widget. That's real protection against a casual scroll — a family member won't stumble on it. It's not protection against someone with your passcode, and at a border it's just another folder.
  • Guided Access (Settings → Accessibility) locks the phone into one app — hand someone a game or a map with Photos unreachable. Genuinely useful for the "can I borrow it" and the child-with-a-game moments, and almost nobody uses it.
  • Screen Time restrictions can hide apps entirely — a blunter tool, but it works for a shared device.
  • What nothing protects: a photo that's still in the library. Every control above limits who reaches Photos; none of them changes what's inside once someone does.

What to do before you hand it over

  • For a repair or a work return: back up, then remove what you'd mind a stranger seeing — or, for a repair, ask whether the shop can work without your passcode (Apple's process often can).
  • For family and favours: Guided Access for the moment; a Hidden-album habit for the long run.
  • For travel and sale: the guides linked above.
  • For all of them: know what's actually in your library. The photos that matter here — the ID you photographed for a form, the card, the password screenshot — are the ones you've forgotten, which is precisely why they're still there. Finding them is the step that makes every other precaution matter.

What's overstated

You'll read that handing your phone to anyone is reckless. It isn't — most repairs are honest, most family members aren't snooping, most favours are just favours. The point isn't suspicion; it's that the cost of a forgotten document photo being seen is high, and the cost of removing it is nothing. You don't have to distrust people to prefer that your passport photo isn't the thing they might scroll past.
